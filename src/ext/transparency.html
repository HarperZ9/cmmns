How CMMNS is measured

Updated 2026-10-23.

Every sign in CMMNS' window carries one of two labels: how often it was right when people checked by hand, or "not measured yet". Below is every sign, the data it was checked on and that data's licence, and how evenly the signs fire whichever side a post targets.

How to read the numbers

  • Precision is the share of signs CMMNS showed that the human annotators of a public research corpus agreed with. It answers "when CMMNS shows this, how often is it right?"
  • Recall is the share of the annotated cases that CMMNS found. CMMNS is built for precision: a wrong sign on an honest reply is the harm it avoids most, so recall is low.
  • Each figure gives the count and a 95% interval (Wilson). A wide interval means few cases were checked.
  • All figures come from a held-out test split (seed radar-eval-2026-10-21). Rules were tuned on a separate split, and the test split was read once, after tuning. Measured 2026-10-23.

One figure per sign

Each sign leads with what its measurement means when you see it. The figure draws the 95% interval as a band and the measured value as a mark; a dashed line is the bar a sign must clear to show. Counts, data and limits sit under "How we know".

How often each sign fires

Precision says how often a sign is right when it shows. The hit rate says how much of the labelled text it finds, and the false-alarm rate how much of the rest it flags. From the two, d' measures how well a sign tells the two apart (0 is chance, 1 is modest, 2 is good), and c, the bias, measures how readily it fires (above 0 means reluctant). Bias here means only that; political side is never an input. Every sign is very conservative: c sits between 2.5 and 3.5 on most rows, and most signs find under 3% of the hostile text. d' and c are shown only where a sign fired at least 20 times.

Only some rows score a sign against its own label: dehumanising, violence and stereotype on Measuring Hate Speech, and threats on Civil Comments. The rest score it against general hostility, which conspiracy framing, motive attribution, absolutist wording and essentialising do not try to detect, so a low d' there says the corpora lack a matching label. The French corpus gave too few hits to measure. Method and caveats: EVALUATION.md, "Hit rate, false alarms, sensitivity and bias".

Word lists derived from labelled data

For Hebrew, Turkish, Korean, Japanese, Persian, Indonesian and Hindi, a script picks each slur term from the training half of public corpora labelled by people, and the other half measures it. A language shows signs only when its list was derived against labels of hate aimed at a group and the 95% interval of its precision starts at 70% or above, from at least 20 signs. Persuasion techniques

Checked on the Propaganda Techniques Corpus v2 (CC BY 4.0), news articles labelled span by span. A technique shows in the window only when its precision is at least 80% and its 95% interval starts at 70% or above, from at least 20 hits. Published systems reach F1 of about 30 to 44 on these techniques, where a confident label is wrong more often than right. Whataboutism needs the whole exchange to judge, so it stays off single replies. Every technique so far sits under the bar.

Documented influence operations

CMMNS carries 98 influence operations from 27 attributed countries, first published between 2018 and 2026, and 1,237 domains and accounts their sources named (1,194 domains). A link or account that a report named shows "Named in a published report", with who the sources say ran it, in each source's own words.

What the counts mean. These are operations that investigators found and published, counted by the year of the first report. The counts follow where people looked: platforms look harder in some places and languages than others, and some governments publish takedowns while others keep them quiet.

Every operation meets the same bar, whatever the country: at least one primary source that was read (a platform report, a research lab, a court or government document, or reporting that names its sources), an operator type that a source itself states, and a cited source for every field. A test enforces this on the data.

The window marks each of them as waiting for a measurement.
  • Same text as other replies, and speaks as local, labelled elsewhere: tested on cases written alongside the rules (below). Measuring them on real replies needs live threads labelled by hand.
  • Live threads: every figure here comes from public research corpora. Threads from X, YouTube, Reddit, Facebook, Instagram and Bluesky still need labelling by hand.
  • French language signs: the French corpus has too few hits to measure. Persuasion cues in French: waiting for a corpus whose licence CMMNS can use.
  • Call for vigilantes: under the 20 hits in the test split that a measurement needs.
  • Site labels (Community Notes, ads, posting app, X's spam label) and matches against cited tables (outlet owners, state links) carry the site's or the cited source's own judgement, and their evidence is that site or source.

Known limits

  • Precision. The corpora come from Reddit, Twitter, Gab, YouTube and news comments, several years old. Two of them were sampled to be rich in hate speech (about 61% hostile). On Civil Comments, where 8% of comments are hostile, about a third of strong labels (23 of 68) fell on text the raters called toxic. Real reply threads sit somewhere between. Some Civil "false positives" are quoted or ironic uses that raters scored below 0.5, so 34% is a floor more than a point estimate.
  • Recall. CMMNS is a hand-built lexicon and grammar, tuned for precision. It finds a small share of hostile text: MHS item recall is under 3% for every family. A quiet thread can still hold hostile replies.
  • Weak families. Conspiracy, absolutist and essentialise describe framing. The corpora label hostility, so their figures show how often the framing lands on hostile text, as context.
  • Extreme. The thread report's extreme count finds about one severe reply in six (17% recall on MHS). Treat it as a floor.
  • Synthetic. The duplicate and mismatch figures come from cases the rules' author wrote and labelled alongside the rules.
  • PTC is news articles from 48 outlets, mostly 2017 to 2019. Replies are shorter and blunter, and precision on them is still to be measured. It could be higher or lower.
  • PTC's annotators marked most uses and missed some. A true use they missed counts against CMMNS Commons, so precision leans low.
  • A technique describes how a post argues, and true claims use techniques too.
